2. Facts necessary to decide the controversy, briefly 
stated, are as hereunder:-
The respondent no.3 Kashmir Kartar Singh (the 
informant) lodged a report against the petitioner and others 
on 07.11.2020 for the offences punishable under Sections 
420, 467, 468, 471 IPC. There are allegations against 
various persons in the FIR. Against the petitioner, it is the 
case in the FIR that based on a forged will of Jogender Kaur, 
forged death certificate was prepared and a portion of her 
land was mutated in the name of the petitioner. The 
informant is power of attorney holder of the co-sharer. It is 
this FIR, in which, after investigation, charge sheet has been 
submitted against various persons. The Investigating Officer 
(for short, “the IO”) concluded that Jogender Kaur was alive. 
In order to grab the Gram Sabha land, a forged will and a 
forged death certificate was prepared and the land was 
mutated in the name of the petitioner and others. Charge 3
sheet under Section 420, 467, 468, 471 and 120B IPC has 
been filed against the petitioner. The record further reveals 
that a proclamation for appearance was issued against the 
petitioner on 01.06.2020. It appears that the petitioner did 
not appear, therefore, the IO approached the concerned 
court for procuring a process under Section 83 of the Code 
of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (for short, “the Code”) on 
30.07.2021. The concerned court issued process under 
Section 83 of the Code against the petitioner on 30.07.2021.
On 21.09.2021, cognizance was taken. The petitioner is 
before this Court now, challenging the summoning orders 
and other processes, as aforesaid.

7. This is a petition under Section 482 of the Code. 
The jurisdiction is invoked to give effect to any order under 
the Code or to prevent the abuse of process of any court or 
otherwise to secure the ends of justice. In case, the offences
are disclosed as a rule generally, interference at this stage is 
not warranted. A legitimate trial is not stopped at its 
threshold. Factual determination is not expected of. The 
jurisdiction under Section 482 of the Code is much wide,
but guided by the principles laid down by the Hon’ble 
Supreme Court. The principles governing the jurisdiction 
has been illustratively laid down by the Hon’ble Supreme 
Court in the case of State of Haryana and Others Vs. Bhajan 
Lal and others, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 335. In para 102 of it, 
the Hon’ble Supreme Court observed as hereunder:-
“102. In the backdrop of the interpretation of the 
various relevant provisions of the Code under Chapter XIV 
and of the principles of law enunciated by this Court in a 
series of decisions relating to the exercise of the 
extraordinary power under Article 226 or the inherent 
powers under Section 482 of the Code which we have 
extracted and reproduced above, we give the following 
categories of cases by way of illustration wherein such 
power could be exercised either to prevent abuse of the 
process of any court or otherwise to secure the ends of 
justice, though it may not be possible to lay down any 
precise, clearly defined and sufficiently channelised and 
inflexible guidelines or rigid formulae and to give an 7
exhaustive list of myriad kinds of cases wherein such 
power should be exercised.
(1) Where the allegations made in the first information 
report or the complaint, even if they are taken at their face 
value and accepted in their entirety do not prima facie 
constitute any offence or make out a case against the 
accused.
(2) Where the allegations in the first information report 
and other materials, if any, accompanying the FIR do not 
disclose a cognizable offence, justifying an investigation by 
police officers under Section 156(1) of the Code except 
under an order of a Magistrate within the purview of 
Section 155(2) of the Code.
(3) Where the uncontroverted allegations made in the 
FIR or complaint and the evidence collected in support of 
the same do not disclose the commission of any offence 
and make out a case against the accused.
(4) Where, the allegations in the FIR do not constitute 
a cognizable offence but constitute only a non-cognizable 
offence, no investigation is permitted by a police officer 
without an order of a Magistrate as contemplated under 
Section 155(2) of the Code.
(5) Where the allegations made in the FIR or complaint 
are so absurd and inherently improbable on the basis of 
which no prudent person can ever reach a just conclusion 
that there is sufficient ground for proceeding against the 
accused.
(6) Where there is an express legal bar engrafted in any 
of the provisions of the Code or the concerned Act (under 
which a criminal proceeding is instituted) to the 
institution and continuance of the proceedings and/or 
where there is a specific provision in the Code or the 
concerned Act, providing efficacious redress for the 
grievance of the aggrieved party.
(7) Where a criminal proceeding is manifestly attended 
with mala fide and/or where the proceeding is maliciously 
instituted with an ulterior motive for wreaking vengeance 
on the accused and with a view to spite him due to private 
and personal grudge.”8
8. Arguments in the instant case have been made 
with regard to the bar, as contained, under Section 195 of 
the Code in taking cognizance. Arguments have been
touched on the aspect of the offences, as covered under 
Section 195 (1) (b) (i) on the one side and Section 195 (1) (b) 
(ii) on the other side. Before the discussion is made further, 
it will be apt to reproduce Section 195 (1) of the Code. It is 
as hereunder:-
“195. Prosecution for contempt of lawful 
authority of public servants, for offences against 
public justice and for offences relating to documents 
given in evidence.— (1) No Court shall take cognizance—
(a) (i) of any offence punishable under sections 172 
to 188 (both inclusive) of the Indian Penal 
Code, (45 of 1860), or 
(ii) of any abetment of, or attempt to commit, 
such offence, or
(iii) of any criminal conspiracy to commit such 
offence, 
except on the complaint in writing of the public servant 
concerned or of some other public servant to whom he is 
administratively subordinate; 
(b) (i) of any offence punishable under any of the 
following sections of the Indian Penal Code 
(45 of 1860), namely, sections 193 to 196 
(both inclusive), 199, 200, 205 to 211 (both 
inclusive) and 228, when such offence is 
alleged to have been committed in, or in 
relation to, any proceeding in any Court, or
(ii) of any offence described in section 463, or 
punishable under section 471, section 475 or 
section 476, of the said Code, when such 
offence is alleged to have been committed in 
respect of a document produced or given in 
evidence in a proceeding in any Court, or9
(iii) of any criminal conspiracy to commit, or 
attempt to commit, or the abetment of, any 
offence specified in sub-clause (i) or sub￾clause (ii), 1 
except on the complaint in writing of that Court or 
by such officer of the Court as that Court may 
authorise in writing in this behalf, or of some other 
Court to which that Court is subordinate.”
9. A bare perusal of Section 195 of the Code reveals 
that sub-Section (1) (b) (i) deals with the offences relating to 
false evidence and offences against public justice, whereas, 
offences under Section 195 (1) (b) (ii) deals with forgery and 
using the false documents as genuine. These are two 
distinct category.
10. In the case of Iqbal Singh Marwah (supra), with 
regard to offences of forgery in such matters, the Hon’ble 
Supreme Court in para 33 categorically held that this bar in 
Section 195 would be applicable only if the offences are 
committed during the time when the document was 
in custodia legis. The Hon’ble Court observed as hereunder:-
“33. In view of the discussion made above, we are of 
the opinion that Sachida Nand Singh [(1998) 2 SCC 493 : 
1998 SCC (Cri) 660] has been correctly decided and the 
view taken therein is the correct view. Section 195(1)(b)(ii) 
CrPC would be attracted only when the offences 
enumerated in the said provision have been committed 
with respect to a document after it has been produced or 
given in evidence in a proceeding in any court i.e. during 
the time when the document was in custodia legis.”10
11. In fact, in the case of Bhima Razu Prasad Vs. 
State, 2021 SCC OnLIne SC 210, Hon’ble Supreme Court 
further made a distinction between Section 195 (1) (b) (i) and 
Section 195 (1) (b) (ii) of the Code. In para 32, the Hon’ble 
Court observed as hereunder:-
“32. This Court thereafter proceeded to distinguish 
between the offence of fabricating false evidence under 
Sections 192 and 193, IPC and the offence of forgery. It 
noted that the averments made by the appellants in their 
complaints pertained exclusively to giving of false evidence 
and did not disclose the ingredients of forgery as defined 
under the IPC. Hence, this Court in Bandekar 
Brothers upheld the respondents' contentions, and opined 
that Iqbal Singh Marwah would not benefit the appellants 
in that case. Even though the false evidence was created 
outside of the Court, it was by the appellants' own 
admission, created “in relation to” proceedings before the 
Court. Thus, this Court held that:
“19. At this stage, it is important to understand the 
difference between the offences mentioned in Section 
195(1)(b)(i) and Section 195(1)(b)(ii) of the Code of Criminal 
Procedure. Where the facts mentioned in a complaint 
attracts the provisions of Section 191 to 193 of the Penal 
Code, 1860, Section 195(1)(b)(i) of the Code of Criminal 
Procedure applies. What is important is that once these 
Sections of the Penal Code, 1860 are attracted, the offence 
should be alleged to have been committed in, or in relation 
to, any proceeding in any Court. Thus, what is clear is 
that the offence punishable under these Sections does not 
have to be committed only in any proceeding in any Court 
but can also be an offence alleged to have been 
committed in relation to any proceeding in any Court.
22. Contrasted with Section 195(1)(b)(i), Section 
195(1)(b)(ii) of the Code of Criminal Procedure speaks of 
offences described in Section 463, and punishable under 
Sections 471, 475 or 476 of the Penal Code, 1860, when 11
such offences are alleged to have been committed in 
respect of a document produced or given in evidence in a 
proceeding in any Court. What is conspicuous by its 
absence in Section 195(1)(b)(ii) are the words “or in 
relation to”, making it clear that if the provisions of 
Section 195(1)(b)(ii) are attracted, then the offence alleged 
to have been committed must be committed in respect of a 
document that is custodia legis, and not an offence that 
may have occurred prior to the document being 
introduced in court proceedings. Indeed, it is this 
distinction that is vital in understanding the sheet anchor 
of the Appellant's case namely, this Court's judgment 
in Iqbal Singh Marwah (supra).”
12. Para 33 of the judgment in the case of Bhima 
Razu (supra) begins with the lines “We fully agree with the 
aforementioned reasoning”. Above law categorically makes it 
clear that insofar as, offences, as enumeration under 
Section 195 (1)(b)(ii) of the Code are concerned, the bar for 
taking cognizance would be applicable only when the 
offence(s) is committed in respect of the documents that are
in custodia legis. Insofar as, the offences enumerated under 
Section 195 (1)(b) (i) of the Code are concerned, it may 
stretch beyond the custodia legis because of the words “or in 
relation to” as contained in that sub Section.
13. In the instant case, as stated, charge sheet has 
been submitted against the petitioner for the offences 
punishable under Sections 420, 468, 471, 120B IPC. These 
offences are not covered under Section 195 (1) (b) (i) of the 12
Code. It relates to cheating, criminal conspiracy, forgery and 
using forged documents as genuine. As stated, with regard 
to forgery and related offences, the bar under Section 195 of 
the Code would be applicable only if the offence is 
committed in respect of the document in custodia legis.
Instant is not such a case. In the instant case, it is the 
categorical case of the prosecution that forged will of 
Jogender Kaur was prepared. Forged death certificate of 
Jogender Kaur was prepared and mutation was got done in 
the name of the petitioner.
14. Learned senior counsel for the petitioner has 
raised arguments with regard to the “expediency” in 
proceeding with the case. A reference has been made to the 
judgment in the case of Iqbal Marwah (supra), particularly 
para 23 of it, has been referred to. In para 23, the Hon’ble 
Supreme Court observed that “in view of the language 
used in Section 340 CrPC the court is not bound to 
make a complaint regarding commission of an offence 
referred to in Section 195(1)(b), as the section is 
conditioned by the words “court is of opinion that it is 
expedient in the interests of justice”. This shows that 
such a course will be adopted only if the interest of 
justice requires and not in every case.”
(emphasis supplied)13
15. Provision of Section 340 of the Code would come 
into play, when the provision of Section 195 of the Code 
would come in operation. It is in such matters that the 
Court has observed that unless it is “expedient in the 
interest of justice”, action should not be taken. In the 
instant case, as stated, the forgery has allegedly been done
when the document was not in custodia legis. Therefore, 
there is no bar under Section 195 (i)(b) (ii) of the Code for 
taking cognizance in the instant case. And, accordingly, 
there is no scope of attracting the provision of Section 340 of 
the Code.
16. Learned senior counsel would raise an argument 
that an IO may not circumvent the procedural protection 
given to a person. This has been argued on the premise that 
if the case as posed by the prosecution is admitted, there are 
shades of offence under Section 193 IPC and it attracts the 
procedural bar under Section 195 (1) (b) (i) of the Code.
17. It is true that according to the prosecution itself, 
it is a case of fabricating false evidence also. It is a case of 
the prosecution that a forged will and forged death 
certificates were prepared for the purpose of mutation. But 
then, charge sheet has not been submitted under Section 
193 IPC. 14
18. The question is that; “Can cognizance under other 
offence (for which bar under Section 195 of the Code does 
not apply) may not be taken, when the same set of facts, 
offence under Section 193 IPC is also made out, but neither 
charge sheet was filed under Section 193 IPC, nor 
cognizance was taken thereunder.
19. The bar is specific to the certain categories of 
cases under Section 195 of the Code. For the offences, in 
which the petitioner has been charge sheeted, there is no 
bar in taking cognizance. Cognizance has been taken 
against the petitioner under Sections 420, 467, 468 and 471 
IPC. This is a case covered under Section 195 (1) (b) (ii) of 
the Code. The documents in respect of which allegedly 
forgery has been committed, were not in custodia legis. If the 
IO did not chose to proceed under other offences, in which 
cases the protection under Section 195 (1) (b) (i) of the Code 
would have been available, it does not vitiate the 
proceedings.
20. The question of “expediency” has been, in fact,
argued in view of the statement of Jogender Kaur recorded 
during investigation, as placed as Annexure 12 to the 
petition. Substantially, Jogender Kaur has proved the 
prosecution case. She has stated that she did not execute 15
any will. She denied her signatures, but she has also stated 
that she does not want to proceed against anyone. It is not 
such a case, in which a victim alone may roll the wheel of 
justice. It is a cognizable offence. Report was made by the 
power of attorney holder of the co-sharer and IO found that 
such offences are committed.
21. An argument has been raised that the processes 
under Sections 82 and 83 of the Code has been issued not 
in accordance with law. The sole argument on this aspect is 
that the petitioner has approached this Court for 
anticipatory bail and on 02.03.2021, the petitioner was 
directed by the Court to appear before the court concerned 
on or before the 22.03.2021 and apply for bail. But, it is 
argued that the due to COVID-19 pandemic, the petitioner 
could not appear. She is a resident of District Bareilly, Uttar 
Pradesh. Whereas, the processes under Sections 82 and 83 
of the Code were issued at her agricultural address in 
Village Bagulia, Thana Jankhaiya, District Udham Singh 
Nagar. 
22. A reference has been made to an appeal preferred 
by the petitioner in the Court of Commissioner Kumaon 
Mandal, Nainital being Appeal No. 47 of 2019-2020, which 
is Annexure 5. In fact, in this appeal, the petitioner has16
recorded her present address as “Village Bagulia, Tehsil 
Khatima, District Udham Singh Nagar”.
23. The record reveals that the petitioner approached 
for anticipatory bail in this Court which was registered as 
ABA No. 47 of 2021, which was decided on 02.03.2021. The 
Court has disposed of the anticipatory bail application with 
the direction that if the petitioner appears before the court 
concerned on or before 22.03.2021 and moves bail 
application, the same shall be decided on the same day. The 
petitioner never appeared before the court.
24. The proclamation to ensure the presence of the 
petitioner was issued by the court concerned on 01.06.2021. 
It is long after, the order on anticipatory bail application was 
passed by this Court. It is issued at the following address of 
the petitioner:
“Ashoka Farm Bagulia, Police Station 
Jhankhaiya, District Udham Singh Nagar. 
25. It appears that the petitioner did not appear even 
thereafter. The IO further approached the court for issuance 
of process under Section 83 of the Code. On 30.07.2021, the 
court of Judicial Magistrate, 1st Class, Khatima, District 
Udham Singh Nagar passed a detailed order and observed 17
that the process under Section 82 of the Code had already 
been served and it had been affixed at the house of the 
petitioner on 06.06.2021, but they are evading arrest. 
Thereafter, the process under Section 83 of the Code has 
been issued.
26. In the instant petition, the petitioner mentioned 
her address of District Bareilly, Uttar Pradesh. But, as 
stated, in an appeal before the court of Commissioner 
Kumaon Mandal, the petitioner herself has recorded her 
address of Village Bagulia, Tehsil Khatima, District Udham 
Singh Nagar. Not only this, there is annexure 6 to this 
petition, which is an application under Order 1 Rule 10 (2) 
of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 of the petitioner filed in 
a Civil Suit No. 1 of 2018, before the court of Civil Judge 
(Senior Division), Khatima, Udham Singh Nagar. In this 
application her address is recorded as Village Bagulia, Tehsil 
Khatima, District Udham Singh Nagar. At the same address, 
process under Section 82 of the Code was issued. It cannot 
be said that it was issued on the wrong address. 
27. In view of the foregoing discussion, this Court 
does not see any reason to make any interference and the 
petition deserves to be dismissed.18
28. The petition is dismissed.
